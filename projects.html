What makes statistical learning efficient? (Dissertation)

People can pick up regularities in a stream of input, such as which syllables tend to follow each other. Learning is often reported to be better for speech than for other kinds of input. My dissertation asks whether this reflects something specific to language, properties of particular stimuli, or more general factors such as familiarity and experience.

Across studies, I compare statistical learning of syllables, tones, shapes, non-linguistic human sounds, and signs, and I am testing how experience with sign language relates to learning from signed input.

Does background music hurt or help speech comprehension?

Background sounds can make speech harder to understand, yet many people choose to listen to music while they work or study. Previous research suggests that music may support learning through reward-related (dopaminergic) mechanisms and/or its effects on mood and arousal. However, most of this evidence comes from studies in which participants learned visually presented verbal material, such as written text, while background music played. Less is known about how music interferes with understanding spoken language, and whether its rewarding aspects might offset that interference. In this project, we ask which properties of music matter by comparing comprehension of short stories presented in silence or with intact, reversed, pitch-scrambled, rhythm-jittered, or pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered versions of the same music.

Causative comprehension in Turkish-speaking children

Causative sentences can be difficult for young learners because they require mapping who caused whom to do what. Evidence from English-speaking children suggests that younger children may rely on the number of nouns in a sentence to interpret causative events, and shift toward relying on the verb's meaning as their verb knowledge grows. Turkish offers a useful test case for this shift because it marks causation transparently with verb morphology. We asked whether Turkish-speaking children rely on the number of nouns, on causative morphology, or both, and at what age any shift occurs. I collected data from 85 children (ages 3–6) at preschools in İzmir, İstanbul, and Bodrum, Turkey.

Are dogs sensitive to musical structure?

Human infants show a listening preference for consonant over dissonant sounds early in life. Dogs offer an interesting comparison: their hearing range overlaps substantially with ours (although it extends to much higher frequencies), and many dogs regularly overhear music, for example from the car radio, from music played at home, or from an owner practicing an instrument. In this project, we replicated Experiment 1 of Trainor and Heinmiller (1998) with dogs, to test whether dogs also respond differently to consonant and dissonant musical intervals. We are currently developing follow-up studies with new stimulus sets.

How much can infants hold in auditory memory?

Understanding speech requires briefly holding sounds in mind as new ones arrive, so auditory short-term memory may play an important role in early language learning. Yet relatively little is known about how much auditory information infants can keep in memory at once.

Ross-Sheehy and Newman (2015) tested this by playing 10-month-old infants repeating sequences of instrument sounds (such as flute, piano, and cello). In some streams, the sequence stayed the same on every repetition; in others, one instrument changed each time. Noticing the change requires remembering every sound in the sequence, so a listening preference for the changing streams can indicate memory for the full sequence. Infants showed this preference for two-instrument sequences but not four-instrument sequences.

We are replicating this study online, which lets families take part from home and allows us to test whether the original findings hold with a remote version of the task.
