What makes statistical learning efficient? (Dissertation)

People can pick up regularities in a stream of input, such as which syllables tend to follow each other. Learning is often reported to be better for speech than for other kinds of input. My dissertation asks whether this reflects something specific to language, properties of particular stimuli, or more general factors such as familiarity and experience.

Across studies, I compare statistical learning of syllables, tones, shapes, non-linguistic human sounds, and signs, and I am testing how experience with sign language relates to learning from signed input.

Does background music hurt or help speech comprehension?

Background sounds can make speech harder to understand, yet many people choose to listen to music while they work or study. In this project, participants listened to short stories in silence or with different versions of the same background music: intact, reversed, pitch-scrambled, rhythm-jittered, or both pitch-scrambled and rhythm-jittered. Comprehension was best in silence, and better with intact music than with music in which both pitch and rhythm were disrupted.

Causative comprehension in Turkish-speaking children

Turkish marks causation with verb morphology. With Yining Nie, Bob Slevc, and Rochelle Newman, I am studying how Turkish-speaking children between ages 3 and 6 comprehend causative sentences, and how this changes with age. Data were collected with families and preschools in Turkey.
